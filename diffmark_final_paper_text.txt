Abstract —The rapid proliferation of deepfake technology poses
significant threats to digital media integrity, enabling malicious
manipulation of facial images with near-indistinguishable results.
Existing watermarking methods often fail to maintain robustness
after deepfake operations such as face-swapping and expression
transfer, as these manipulations substantially alter the spatial
and frequency characteristics of images. In this paper, we
present DiffMark, a diffusion-based watermarking framework
specifically designed to withstand deepfake attacks. DiffMark
leverages a U-Net-based encoder-decoder architecture tightly
coupled with a guided diffusion process to embed imperceptible
binary watermark messages into facial images. A key innovation
is the use of multiple deepfake noise layers during training,
including SimSwap, StarGAN, UniFace, CSCS, and FSRT, en-
abling the model to learn robust watermark representations that
survive diverse manipulation pipelines. We further introduce a
guidance mechanism that directs the diffusion sampling process
toward enhanced message fidelity under deepfake perturbation.
Extensive experiments on CelebA-HQ and LFW datasets at
resolutions of 128 ×128 and 256 ×256 demonstrate that DiffMark
achieves state-of-the-art bit accuracy and perceptual quality,
outperforming prior watermarking methods in both robustness
and invisibility metrics.

I. I NTRODUCTION
The emergence of Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs)
and latent diffusion models has dramatically lowered the bar-
rier to creating convincing deepfake content. Face-swapping
systems such as SimSwap [8] and expression-transfer methods
such as FSRT [12] can produce facial images that are visually
indistinguishable from authentic photographs, creating serious
risks for misinformation, identity fraud, and non-consensual
media generation.
Digital watermarking offers a proactive defence strategy: by
embedding an imperceptible ownership or provenance signal
into an image before distribution, one can later verify whether
the image has been manipulated. However, deepfake opera-
tions constitute a particularly challenging class of distortions
because they jointly modify spatial structures, textures, and
semantic content. Classical watermarking methods, designed
to withstand compression, resizing, or JPEG artefacts, cannot
reliably survive face-swapping pipelines that replace large
facial regions.Why diffusion-based embedding is inherently more ro-
bust than GAN-based watermarking. A GAN-based encoder
embeds a message in a single forward pass, mapping the host
image and message jointly to a watermarked image through
one deterministic function. Because this mapping is one-shot,
the watermark signal tends to concentrate in whichever spatial-
frequency band the encoder network finds most convenient to
modify, and any transformation that perturbs that particular
band, such as a face-swap network replacing an entire facial
region, can remove most of the embedded signal in one
operation. A diffusion-based encoder, in contrast, embeds the
message incrementally across a multi-step denoising trajectory,
injecting the message embedding into every residual block
at every timestep via adaptive group normalisation. This has
two consequences for robustness. First, the watermark is
distributed across multiple noise scales and spatial resolu-
tions rather than concentrated in one, so a manipulation that
damages high-frequency detail still leaves lower-frequency,
coarser-scale traces of the message intact, and vice versa.
Second, because the diffusion process is itself a model of
how images can be perturbed and reconstructed, the encoder
implicitly learns a representation that is already compatible
with reconstruction-style edits, which is analogous to the kind
of edit a face-swap or reenactment network performs on a local
region. GAN-based encoders have no equivalent inductive bias
and must instead rely entirely on the differentiable noise layers
seen during training to discover robust features. We stress that
this is a structural argument for why diffusion embedding
is a good fit for the problem, not a claim that diffusion
watermarks are unconditionally unbreakable. The limitations
discussion later in this paper addresses an important counter-
example, diffusion-based regeneration attacks, where the same
generative flexibility that helps embedding robustness can also
be turned into an attack that erases the mark.
Recent learning-based watermarking approaches [1]–[4]
have significantly improved robustness by training encoder-
decoder networks end-to-end against differentiable noise lay-
ers. Yet none of these methods explicitly models the complex,
non-linear distortions introduced by modern deepfake genera-
tors, limiting their effectiveness in practice.
In this work we propose DiffMark, which addresses this gap
through three contributions:

•A diffusion-process-based embedding architecture that

II. R ELATED WORK
A. Traditional Watermarking
Early watermarking techniques operated in transform do-
mains such as DCT [15] and DWT, encoding bits into
frequency coefficients. While resilient to common signal-
processing distortions, they are unable to tolerate semantic-
level editing, including face swapping.
B. Learning-Based Watermarking
HiDDeN [1] introduced the first end-to-end trainable
encoder-decoder framework, pairing the encoder with a differ-
entiable noise layer during training. StegaStamp [2] extended
this to physical-world scenarios using spatial transformer net-
works. SepMark [3] disentangles robust and fragile watermark
components, while LampMark [4] employs localized attention
pooling to improve spatial fidelity. None of these methods
directly targets deepfake distortions.
C. Deepfake Detection and Provenance
Passive deepfake detectors [14] analyse artefacts in image
statistics to classify images as real or fake. Active methods
embed a signal at capture time so that downstream forgery
is detectable regardless of detector evolution. DiffMark is
an active method: it does not classify deepfakes but instead
enables ownership verification and tampering localisation.
D. Diffusion Models
Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models (DDPMs) [5]
learn to reverse a Markov noising chain. Guided diffusion
[7] introduced classifier guidance to steer generation toward
desired attributes. DiffMark repurposes this guidance mech-
anism to maximise watermark message recovery rather than
class probability.
E. Recent Diffusion-Based and Provenance Watermarking
(2024–2026)
A growing body of work has moved robust watermarking
toward diffusion architectures and, in parallel, toward system-
level provenance standards. Zhang et al. [16] embed watermarkbits directly into a pretrained Stable Diffusion backbone rather
than a bespoke encoder, trading some training flexibility for
the ability to reuse a strong pretrained prior. Wang et al. [17]
take a complementary route to ours, using facial-landmark
structure to build a perceptual watermark tailored to face-
swap forensics rather than a generic deepfake noise layer. Tan
et al. [18] propose WaterDiff, which repurposes a pretrained
diffusion autoencoder for reversible image-to-latent mapping
and embeds the message via a wavelet-domain transform of the
latent; unlike DiffMark, its reliance on an invertible mapping
tends to trade away fine image detail, whereas our residual-
based embedding (Eq. 2) is designed to preserve it. Wen et
al. [19] introduced Tree-Ring watermarks, which fingerprint
the initial noise pattern of a diffusion sampler rather than
the output image, giving strong robustness for content that
is itself diffusion-generated but which does not directly apply
to watermarking an already-captured photograph, as DiffMark
does. Zhang et al. [20] and its 2025 successor OmniGuard [21]
extend versatile watermarking to joint tamper-localisation and
copyright protection, which is a complementary goal to the
pure bit-accuracy objective studied here. Lu et al. [22] present
VINE, a diffusion-encoder watermark evaluated at ICLR 2025
that is explicitly benchmarked for robustness to generative
image editing; together with an updated TrustMark [23], these
represent the current state of the art in generic (non-deepfake-
specific) learned watermarking against which DiffMark could
usefully be benchmarked in future work (Section VII).
On the provenance side, the Coalition for Content Prove-
nance and Authenticity finalised C2PA specification 2.2 in
2025 [25], which complements pixel-level watermarking with
cryptographically signed manifests, and Google’s SynthID-
Image [26] deployed learned watermarking of generated im-
ages at internet scale. These standards address a different
threat model from DiffMark: they focus on labelling AI-
generated content and platform-level metadata, whereas Diff-
Mark focuses on surviving an active deepfake attack on
an already-watermarked photograph. The two approaches are
complementary, however, and could in principle be combined,
with a DiffMark-style pixel watermark serving as the soft-
binding signal referenced in the C2PA specification.
We also note a concurrent and independently developed
system that shares our method’s name and general approach:
Sun et al. [27], first released in mid-2025, also propose a
diffusion-conditioned watermark trained against a deepfake
noise layer. Their design uses a frozen autoencoder to sim-
ulate deepfake manipulation during training and a related
deepfake-resistant guidance term, whereas the present work
trains directly against five named deepfake systems plus an
LDM-autoencoder proxy and an “ordinary” distortion set, and
uses a message-conditioned adaptive group-normalisation U-
Net rather than a frozen autoencoder. We flag the overlap
explicitly for the reader’s benefit and view the convergence
of two independent groups on similar architectural choices as
supporting evidence that diffusion-conditioned embedding is a
promising direction for deepfake-robust watermarking, rather
than as a conflict between the two papers.

III. L ITERATURE REVIEW AND RESEARCH GAPANALYSIS
Section II surveyed individual prior works; this section
synthesises that body of work thematically and identifies the
specific gap that motivates DiffMark’s design.
The watermarking literature has progressed through three
broad generations. The first generation (transform-domain
methods such as DCT- and DWT-based spread-spectrum wa-
termarking [15]) offers mathematically well-understood ro-
bustness guarantees against signal-processing operations, but
was never designed to survive semantic, content-altering edits,
and consequently collapses under any transformation that
touches the underlying facial identity. The second generation
(end-to-end learned encoder-decoder frameworks such as HiD-
DeN [1], StegaStamp [2], SepMark [3], and LampMark [4])
replaced hand-designed transforms with differentiable noise
layers and learned features, substantially improving robustness
to common distortions such as JPEG compression, cropping,
and blurring. However, as Table I summarises, none of these
methods trains against, or evaluates on, deepfake-specific
manipulations, so their robustness to face-swapping and reen-
actment is, at best, incidental. The third generation , diffusion-
based and provenance-oriented approaches [16]–[23], [25]–
[27], is the most recent and the most directly relevant to this
work. Within this generation, three distinct sub-strategies are
visible: (i) repurposing a pretrained diffusion backbone for wa-
termark embedding [16], [18], (ii) fingerprinting the diffusion
sampling process itself rather than the output pixels [19], and
(iii) building generic, non-deepfake-specific robustness against
arbitrary generative edits [22], [23]. A parallel, complementary
track pursues system-level provenance through cryptographic
manifests and platform-scale watermarking [25], [26] rather
than pixel-level robustness at all.
Taken together, the literature reveals a consistent gap: meth-
ods that are robust to conventional signal-processing distor-
tions are not trained or evaluated against deepfake manipula-
tion, while methods that do target diffusion-based robustness
either operate on already-generated content (Tree-Ring [19]),
embed within a diffusion backbone without deepfake-specific
supervision [16], [18], or pursue generic, non-deepfake robust-
ness [22], [23]. Only Wang et al. [17] and the concurrent work
of Sun et al. [27] directly target deepfake robustness with a
diffusion-adjacent design, and neither trains against as broad
a set of named, heterogeneous face-manipulation systems
as the five used here. DiffMark is positioned to close this
gap by combining a diffusion-native embedding architecture
with supervision from five distinct deepfake systems plus an
autoencoder proxy, and by adding a message-guided sam-
pling mechanism that specifically targets bit-accuracy recovery
rather than tamper localisation or generic edit robustness.

VI. D ISCUSSION
A. Why Diffusion Helps
The iterative denoising process distributes the watermark
signal across multiple feature scales. This multi-scale embed-
ding makes it difficult for face-swapping systems to destroy
the mark without introducing perceptible artefacts.
B. Limitations and Failure Cases
DiffMark requires access to deepfake model weights during
training for the noise layer. Newly released deepfake systems
may degrade accuracy until the noise layer set is updated.
Fig. 11. Leave-one-out ablation showing the drop in bit accuracy when each

Guided sampling partially mitigates this, as shown by the gap
between seen- and unseen-attack accuracy narrowing from 9.2
points without guidance to 3.4 points at s= 2.0in Table V;
however, that gap does not close to zero, and the true failure
mode is broader than a single unseen face-swap network.
The most severe failure case we can identify from the
literature, rather than from a new experiment run for this
revision, is diffusion-based image regeneration. Ni et al. [24]
show that passing a watermarked image through a general
diffusion-based image-to-image regeneration pipeline, as op-
posed to a task-specific face-swap network, can erase learned
watermarks, including ones designed for robustness, because
regeneration effectively resynthesises the entire image from
a diffusion prior rather than locally editing a face region.
DiffMark’s training set includes an LDM/VQ-GAN autoen-
coder pass as one noise layer, which should offer partial
protection, but this differs from a full multi-step diffusion
regeneration attack and was not evaluated against one in this
manuscript. We view closing this gap, either empirically by
adding a diffusion-regeneration noise layer, or theoretically
=== PAGE 8 ===
by characterising the regime in which distributed multi-scale
embedding still survives resynthesis, as the most important
open failure case, and we list it as the first item in Section VII
rather than claim a result we have not measured.
A second class of failure case is severe image degrada-
tion compounded with a deepfake attack (e.g., heavy JPEG
compression or downscaling applied before or after face-
swapping). The training noise set includes an “Ordinary” cate-
gory (JPEG, Gaussian noise, blurring) sampled independently
from the deepfake layers, but the two are never composed
together during training in the setup described in Section III-
D, so robustness to a compounded degradation-then-attack
pipeline is untested and should not be assumed to match the
single-distortion numbers in Table III.
C. Computational Cost
The DDIM-10 schedule limits embedding overhead to ap-
proximately 0.3 s per image on an A100 GPU, which is
acceptable for offline pipelines but potentially limiting for real-
time applications.
VII. S UGGESTED FUTURE EXPERIMENTS
The following experiments were suggested during review.
None were run for this revision; we list them here as a
concrete, prioritised agenda rather than fabricate results for
experiments not actually performed.
•Robustness against diffusion-based image regeneration
attacks (highest priority; see the limitations discussion
in Section VI-B).
•Cross-dataset evaluation on FaceForensics++, FFHQ, and
DFDC, in addition to the CelebA-HQ / LFW results
reported here.
•Statistical significance testing (multiple training seeds /
multiple evaluation runs) for the headline numbers in
Tables III–VI, which currently report single-run results.
•Robustness against sequential/composed attacks: JPEG
compression, resizing, and cropping combined with a
deepfake pass, and multiple chained deepfake manipu-
lations.
•Head-to-head comparison with recent diffusion-based wa-
termarking methods, specifically VINE [22] and an up-
dated TrustMark [23], under a shared evaluation protocol.
•Runtime and memory profiling against competing meth-
ods, including an actual measured memory footprint at
both resolutions.
•Ablation on the number of deepfake noise layers used
in training (e.g., 3 vs. 5 vs. 7 systems) and on message
length at fixed resolution, to separate the two confounded
factors in the current Table II configurations.
•A latent-diffusion variant of the encoder-decoder, for di-
rect comparison against the pixel-space guided-diffusion
design used here.
VIII. C ONCLUSION
We presented DiffMark, a diffusion-based watermarking
framework designed to survive deepfake manipulation. Bycoupling watermark embedding with guided diffusion and
training against diverse deepfake noise layers, DiffMark
achieves over 91% average bit accuracy across five deepfake
systems while maintaining high perceptual quality (PSNR
>37 dB, SSIM >0.97). Message-guided sampling further
boosts robustness against unseen attacks, and ablations confirm
that each design choice contributes meaningfully to overall
performance. This revision clarifies the derivation of the guid-
ance term, situates the method against 2024–2026 diffusion-
based and provenance watermarking work through a dedicated
literature review, and is explicit about the failure cases and
experiments that remain open, most importantly, robustness to
diffusion-based regeneration attacks.
